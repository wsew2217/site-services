What happened

The customer sent us better data: full street addresses and, for the first time, how many people actually work at each site. We rebuilt the entire model on it. The design survived contact with better data almost untouched, which is the strongest validation the model has had.

The five things that matter

1. Their demand did not grow. Their estate did.

The new extract looks like a 12% jump in tickets. It is not. Duplicate rows inflated the raw file; real observed demand is flat year over year. What actually grew is the footprint: 80 more sites and 3 more countries now in scope. Headcount moves from 127 to 132 people (114 technicians), and that increase is coverage for new sites, not inefficiency.

2. The structure held. That is the headline.

With 97% of sites now precisely placed, every one of the 28 original hub choices came out unchanged when re-derived. Six teams resized by a head or two. One structural change: Peru gets its own campus, because Lima now has more daily on-site work than several countries that already have one, and it was being served from Santiago, 2,500 km and a customs border away. If the customer asks "did the new data break your model," the answer is no, it confirmed it.

3. We can now answer the ratio question, and it favors us.

114 technicians against 51,680 users works out to one technician per 453 users. That number is derived from their call volume, their geography, and time on task, not picked from a benchmark chart. The customer staffs above that today, meaning they currently pay for presence, not work. Our number is the conservative middle: far leaner than staffing sites the old way (even covering just a quarter of sites one-deep would take 247 people), but deliberately fatter than the end state, on purpose.

4. The real project is behavior, not logistics.

Users are used to a technician within walking distance, so they go to the person, not the service desk or self-service. That habit is the biggest risk to the transformation. The 114 is sized to hold service steady on day one while those habits shift, then step down by rule as the data proves demand is moving to healthier channels. Nobody loses coverage; the model shrinks itself as adoption grows.

5. There is a visible road map, and it is a shift, not a cut.

As adoption matures: roughly 90 technicians at 750 users each (the proposed long-term goal), 74 at 1,000, and the low sixties at the theoretical limit. Getting there is not just removing labor; shipping, depots, smart-hands dispatch and OEM warranty usage go up while remote-first fixes and consolidated stock control offset it. The end state is zero-touch: Intune Autopilot plus Dell's global Life Cycle Hub, where a new PC or a refresh is a shipping event with no technician involved. That end state is also new Dell business (OEM warranty attach, LCH), not just fewer bodies.

What we need from the customer

Addresses for the 60 sites missing from their extract, the ticket split by incident vs request (the last modeled assumption), and a green light to stand up Lima.

Same design, sharper numbers, and for the first time a complete story: where they are, where we start, where it can go, and what it takes to get there.
