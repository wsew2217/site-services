1. It started as a simple spreadsheet

Reference Deal A needed a global field-services staffing model: roughly 900 sites, more than 100 countries, and one extract of ticket counts. No addresses. No user counts. No second data source of any kind. Historically, just mapping an estate like this takes days of manual lookups and still comes back inaccurate, and sizing it would be weeks of spreadsheet work that one person could defend and nobody could reproduce.

2. Then I taught the AI my methodology

This is the part that matters. I did not ask Claude for "a staffing model." I taught it mine, twenty-plus years of field services experience turned into rules it could execute: how demand converts to daily workload, what a technician can actually absorb on-site versus remote, when a site deserves local hands versus dispatch, how customs borders and language break a coverage map, why geography sets floors that no ratio can ignore. Claude contributed the speed and the math; the judgment in the model is ours. That distinction is why the output survives customer scrutiny.

3. Several iterations later, a complete solution set

What came out of those working sessions was not a spreadsheet. It was a solution: a virtual campus operating model (one accountable team per site, three routed queues), two boardroom-quality decks (customer and internal), two reconciled Excel workbooks, a formal methodology document, and a world map of the footprint. Every site geocoded and clustered against drive-time reach, the thing that used to take days and never came back accurate, done in an afternoon, repeatably. And underneath it all, a reusable engine: the entire model regenerates from a blank intake template, which means the next account starts at 80% done.

4. We presented it. The customer asked for more.

Deal A was impressed enough with the first pass that they did the thing you always hope a customer does: they invested in it. They pulled the data we told them would sharpen the model, full street addresses and user counts for the entire estate, and sent it back.

A customer handing you better data is a customer who has already bought the approach.

5. The refresh proved the model, and caught what the customer missed

When the new extract arrived, we rebuilt the entire model on it in a single working session. Same day, every deliverable regenerated and reconciled. Three things came out of that:

We caught their data errors.

The raw file showed demand up 12%. It was not; duplicate rows inflated it. Real demand was flat. We walked into the conversation knowing their data better than they did.

The model held.

With 97% of sites now precisely placed, all 28 original hub choices re-derived unchanged. One structural change, Peru earning its own campus, was the model doing exactly what we designed it to do: follow the demand.

We answered the question they had not asked yet.

With user counts in hand, we showed the derived staffing ratio (453 users per technician), proved they are overstaffed today, and laid a road map to 750:1 and beyond, framed honestly as a cost shift into logistics, OEM warranty, and ultimately zero-touch delivery through Intune and Dell's Life Cycle Hub, which is new Dell business, not just fewer bodies.

6. Where this goes next

The customer owes us the last data pieces (60 missing addresses, ticket-type splits), and the request package is ready. The real next build is the true cost model: transition costs, logistics and depot economics, OEM warranty attach, severance and ramp curves, so the road map becomes a priced business case instead of a direction. The pipeline we built makes that a build, not a research project.

The point

One person, with AI as a force multiplier, produced in days what would normally take a team weeks: a customer-validated global operating model, a defensible staffing number, a transformation story, and a reusable engine for every account after this one. The methodology is ours. The speed is new. That combination, expert judgment executed at machine speed, is the art of the possible, and it is repeatable.
